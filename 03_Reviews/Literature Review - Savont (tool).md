## Research question
> What question is this article trying to ask?

The purpose of the article (development of the tool) was to develop a tool that can be used to recover Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) from modern long read amplicons with a high accuracy. This includes reads generated by Oxford Nanopore technology (ONT). 

## Main argument
> Main claim or statement / thesis

- Savont can recover ASVs from modern long read amplicons with a mean accuracy of >98%
- The required sequencing depth that Savont needs is 5 to 16 times lower than the standard tools currently in use, and can generate 95x more ASVs for complex enviroments.
## Methods
> What methods are used to answer the question

### Savont algorithm
The first thing Savont does is generating "SNPmers". This is done by looking at a pair of k-mers, and when the middle base differs, they are both an SNPmer. They give a clear example of this:
AACAA and AAAAA are SNPmers (middle base differs). Many of the k-mers within a sample would be classified as a SNPmer due to sequencing errors, Savont filters out these errors using techniques that are comparable to variant calling methods.

It starts by counting all k-mers in a sample (with a default k-value of 17), and removes all k-mers that are not seen in both strands. It then groups all k-mers into buckets with the same lateral k-1 bases (but possibly different middle bases). The 2 k-mers with the highest frequency are considered possibly qualified to become SNPmers. Savont will apply two tests on these:
1. Strand-bias test using Fisher's exact test
2. Binomial test to filter out low frequency k-mers

The long reads are filtered and indexed by Savont, (filtering based on length and quality). The default (but change is possible) assumes the length of full-length 16s rRNA amplicons are >= 1100 bp and <= 2000 bp. The retained reads are indexed using the SNPmers, with the requirement that the middle SNPmer base has a quality > 25.

Savont performs 2 rounds of clustering:
1. All reads are processed into primary clusters of about 95% identity.
2. Processes each primary cluster seperately

Clustering is done with this structure because highly divergente sequences may not have any shared SNPmers. And if 2 sequences share no SNPmers, then the second clustering step will not work.

I won't go into detail about how to clustering works, as I do not think it is relevant for the project.

For each cluster generated a SIMD partial order alignment is used to create a general consensus sequence (An ASV). 
Some of the consensus sequences found in the previous step will be of low quality. Savont tries to gauge the quality of a consensus sequence by aligning the cluster's reads back and evaluating the alignment statistics. Low quality consensuses will have poorer statistics and will be filtered out. It will then merge similar sequences, filter out chimeras, and generate a final ASV quantification.

These resulting ASVs can be used by taxonomic profilers. But Savont also implements two classification algorithms:
- One that is minimap2 based
- Edgar's sintax algorithm

### Methods

### Introduction
Finding exact marker sequences in samples can lead to taxonomic information (16S rRNA can be used for taxonomic profiling). One of the issues that arises is separating true variation from sequencing noise, at single-nucleotide precision in these markers.

Most current long-read ASV pipelines are forced to use a denoising algorithm, usually designed for shorter reads. This denoising is based on finding error-free reads, within sets of reads. But as the reads increase in size, so will the chance of an error happening. Current methods are inclined to fail on low-depth samples or amplicons with a low-abundance.

Savont uses a different approach: "Cluster-and-consensus". This works differently by not assuming there are error-free reads.
(Shaw et al., 2026)
